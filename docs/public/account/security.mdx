# Secure your account

Your account's security is made up of several separate controls, and each does
a different job:

- A password or an external identity proves that you are the User.
- MFA makes that proof stronger.
- A Session keeps one signed-in app or browser active.
- A Desktop Registration records a public key you have chosen to trust.
- A <Term id="personal-access-token">Personal Access Token</Term> is a
  credential for automation.

It helps to keep them apart in your head. If you think of them all as one
“login token”, it is easy to miss what happens when you revoke one of them.

## Choose and change a password

Proctor follows NIST SP 800-63B. You don't have to mix particular kinds of
character, and passwords don't expire on a schedule. Length matters most.
Proctor refuses a new password, with a message explaining why, when it:

- is shorter or longer than the limits set for your installation;
- is one character repeated, a short repeated pattern such as `abcabc…`, a
  run such as `abcdef…` or `98765…`, or a row of the keyboard such as
  `qwerty…`;
- is on a built-in list of common passwords, in any mix of upper and lower
  case, or is one of those passwords followed only by digits and symbols
  (`sunshine2026!`);
- contains your username, the part of your email address before `@`, or your
  name;
- matches one of your recent passwords. Your operator decides how many are
  remembered (five by default). The check happens on the Proctor server, and
  your password is never sent to an outside breach-checking service.

The same rules apply when your account is created, when you accept an
Invitation, and when you set, change or reset a password. A passphrase of
several unrelated words is the easiest way to meet them.

To change your password from your account, you need a Session where you
signed in recently and strongly. The browser you use to make the change stays
signed in. When the change goes through:

- every other Session on your account is signed out;
- any unused password-reset link stops working;
- a "password changed" notice goes to your account's email address.

## Undo an email change you didn't expect

When the email address on your account changes, Proctor emails the previous
address. The message contains a single-use **Undo this change** link, which
works for a limited time after the change (72 hours by default).

If you didn't expect the change, open the link. The page shows the previous
address it will put back, the new address partly hidden, and when the change
happened. Choose **Undo change** to go ahead. Proctor then:

- puts the previous address back and marks it as verified, because opening
  the link proved you can read that mailbox;
- signs out every Session on the account;
- cancels any pending verification and password-reset links for the address
  that was replaced;
- records the undo in the audit history.

If another account now uses the previous address, Proctor refuses the undo.
Contact your institution if that happens. The link never signs you in.
Afterwards, sign in again, and reset your password if you need to.

## Add MFA and keep recovery codes offline

Setting up MFA takes two steps. First you start the setup, then you turn it on
by entering a valid code. Until you have finished the second step, MFA isn't
protecting you.

Keep your recovery codes offline, and somewhere separate from your password.
Each recovery code works once. If you create a new set, the old set stops
working.

Your account security page shows when you last changed your password, how
many recovery codes you have left out of a full set, and how long a fresh
confirmation lasts on this installation. Turning off MFA and creating new
recovery codes both need a Session that is recent or strong enough for that
action. If your Session is too old, sign in again through the normal account
pages. A Personal Access Token can't be used instead.

## Check your sign-in methods before removing one

Your account can have a local password and one or more external identities.
You connect a provider while signed in, through browser pages made for that
one purpose. Proctor links the provider's fixed identifier for you. It never
picks an account by matching email addresses.

Before you remove a password or unlink a provider, make sure you will still
have another method you can use under the current Access Policy. Proctor won't
let you remove your last usable method.

Unlinking one external identity signs out only the Sessions that were signed in
through that exact identity. That includes Desktop Sessions you approved from
one of those Web Sessions. Your other provider identities aren't affected.

## Review and sign out Sessions

The Session list is a security record. It isn't a list of devices guessed from
browser details. To decide whether you expect a Session, look at when it was
created, when it was last seen, how it signed in, what type of app it is, and
whether it has been revoked.

- Revoke a single Session when you no longer trust that browser or app.
- Revoke all of your Sessions if you think someone has stolen your sign-in
  details. Your current browser will be signed out too.
- An administrator can revoke Sessions for a User who is disabled or whose
  account has been taken over. That doesn't delete the User or their audit
  history.

A revoked Session stays revoked. Closing a browser window is not the same as
revoking the Session on the server.

## Desktop Registrations are permanent records

A Desktop Registration ties together one User, one Institution and the
thumbprint of one P-256 public key. The matching private key stays in the
Desktop app. The account page doesn't show the public JWK or the thumbprint on
purpose. It shows what you need to review the registration: the device, the
platform, the build, when it was created, when it was last used, and whether
it has been revoked.

Revoking a Desktop Registration can't be undone. It revokes every Session and
credential tied to that registration. To use that device again, you need to
approve it again in the browser, and Desktop creates a new key. Proctor
doesn't offer an “undo”, and it never quietly recreates a revoked
registration.

## Keep Personal Access Tokens limited

Personal Access Tokens are bearer credentials for automation, and they only
work on actions that allow them. They are not Web Sessions. They can't be
used for actions that need a Session or a recent sign-in, and they never work
for taking an Exam in Desktop.

You can copy a token's value only once, when you create it. Store it in a
secret manager. Revoke it when you stop using the integration, or if you think
it may have been exposed.

## If you think your account has been compromised

1. From a browser you trust, at your Institution's Proctor address, revoke the
   affected Session, Desktop Registration or Personal Access Token.
2. If you aren't sure what was affected, revoke all of your Sessions and
   change your local password, or recover the affected external provider
   account. If your account's email address was changed without your
   permission, use the undo link sent to the previous address first.
3. Create new MFA recovery codes if someone may have read the ones you stored.
4. Ask an administrator to look at the relevant audit events, by time and by
   what was affected.
5. Never attach credentials, raw provider data, Exam answers or large amounts
   of browser data to your report.
